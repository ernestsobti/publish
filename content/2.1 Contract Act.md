## Contract Law before Indian Contract Act, 1872

During the period of Mauryas, 
- contracts were in the form of "<u>bilateral transactions</u>" 
- ==which were based on free consent on all the terms and conditions involved.==


“During the Mughal rule in India,
- contracts were governed by Mohammedan Law of Contract.
- In this law, the Arabic word ‘Aqd’ is known for contract which means a conjunction.
- In the same way, word ‘Ijab’ was used for proposal and
- ‘Qabul’ was used for acceptance.”


“Hindu law is basically different from that of English law.
- Hindu **law is actually the compilation** of numerous customs and works of Smritikaras, 
- who interpreted and analysed Vedas to develop the various aspect of Hindu law. 
- According to Hindu law, 
	- minor, 
	- intoxicated person,
	- old man or handicapped cannot enter into a valid contract. 
- According to Narada smriti, someone of age upto 8 years is considered as an infant. 
- Age from 8 years to 16 years is considered as boyhood and 
- after 16 years the person is competent to enter into a contract.”

“During British period; 
- before the advent[^1] of the Indian Contract Act, 
- the English Law was applied in the Presidency Towns of Madras, Bombay and Calcutta under the Charter of 1726 issued by king George to the East India Company. 
- If one of the parties of contract is from either of the religion and other is from other religion then the law of the defendant is to be used. 
- This was followed in the presidency towns, but in cities outside the presidency towns, 
- the matters were solved on the basis of justice, equity and good conscience. 
- This procedure was followed till the Indian Contract Act was implemented in India.”


## The Law of Contract : Introduction


- “The Act mostly deals with the general principles and rules governing contracts. 
- The Act is divisible into two parts. 
	- The first part (Section 1-75) deals with the <u>general principles</u> of the law of contract, and therefore applies to all contracts irrespective of their nature. 
	- The second part (Sections 124-238) deals with certain <u>special kinds of contracts</u>, e.g., Indemnity and guarantee, bailment, pledge, and agency.”


## What is Contract 

“The contract consists of two essential elements:
(i) an agreement, and 
(ii) its enforceability by law.”


### Agreement 

“The term ‘agreement’ given in Section 2(e) of the Act is defined as- 
- “every promise and every set of promises, <u>forming the consideration</u> for each other”.”


### Enforceability by Law 

“An agreement to become a contract <u>must give rise to a legal obligation</u> which means a <u>duly enforceable by law</u>.”


### Difference between Agreement and Contract


| Basis of Difference  | Agreement                                                                                                                | Contract                                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Meaning**          | Every <u>promise and every set of promises</u>, forming the consideration for each other. <br> (Promise + Consideration) | An agreement enforceable by law. <br> *(Agreement + Legal Enforceability)*                                |
| **Scope**            | It is a <u>wider term</u> including both <u>legal and social </u>agreements.                                             | It is used in a <u>narrow sense</u> and <u>includes only legally enforceable</u> agreements.              |
| **Legal Obligation** | It <u>may or may not create a legal obligation</u>. An agreement does not always grant rights to the parties.            | It <u>necessarily creates a legal obligation.</u> A contract always grants certain rights to every party. |
| **Nature**           | All agreements are not contracts.                                                                                        | All contracts are agreements.                                                                             |



## Essentials of Valid Contract 


### Not given by Section 10 but are also considered essential


#### Two Parties 

- One cannot <u>contract with himself</u>. 
- A contract <u>involves at least two parties</u> one party making the offer and the other party accepting it. 
- A contract may be made  by <u>natural persons</u> and by <u>other persons having legal existence</u> e.g. companies,  universities etc. 
- It is necessary to remember that <u>identity of the parties be  ascertainable</u>.


#### Parties must be intend to create legal obligations 

- “There must be an <u>intention on the part of the parties to create legal relationship</u> between them. 
- <u> Social or domestic type of agreements are not enforceable</u> in court of law and hence they do not result into contracts.”


#### Other Formalities to be complied with in certain cases

- “A contract may be written or spoken. 
- As to legal effects, there is no difference between a written contract and contract made by word of mouth.
- But in the <u>interest of the parties the contract must be written.</u> 
- In case of certain contracts <u>some other formalities have to be complied</u> with to make an agreement legally enforceable.”
#### Certainty of meaning 
The agreement must be <u>certain</u> and <u>not vague</u> or <u>indefinite</u>.”

#### Possibility of performance of an agreement

- The terms of agreement should be <u>capable of performance</u>. 
- An agreement to <u>do an act impossible in itself cannot be enforced</u>.”

### According to Section 10 

#### Offer and Acceptance or an agreement: 
- An agreement is the <u>first essential element of a valid contract</u>.
- According to <u>Section 2(e) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, “Every promise and every set of promises, forming consideration for each other, is an agreement</u>” 
- and according to <u>Section 2(b) “A proposal when accepted, becomes a promise</u>”. 
- An agreement is an outcome of offer and acceptance for consideration.”


#### Free Consent: 
- Two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the <u>same thing in the same sense. </u>
- This can also be understood as <u>identity of minds</u> in understanding the terms viz <u>consensus ad idem</u>.
- Further such consent must be free.
- Consent would be considered as free consent if it is <u>not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake.</u>”

#### Capacity of the parties: 
- Capacity to contract means the legal ability of a person to enter into a valid contract. 
- Section 11 of the Indian Contract Act specifies that every person is competent to 
- contract who 
	- (a) is of the <u>age of majority</u> according to the law to which he is subject and 
	- (b) is of <u>sound mind</u> and 
	- (c) is <u>not otherwise disqualified</u> from contracting <u>by any law</u> to which he is subject.”

#### “Consideration
- It is referred to as ‘<u>quid pro quo</u>’ i.e. ‘something in return’.
- A valuable consideration in the sense of law may consist either in <u>some right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to one party</u>, 
- or <u>some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other</u>



#### “Lawful Consideration and Object: 

- The <u>consideration and object</u>[^2] of the agreement <u>must be lawful</u>.”
- “Section 23 states that consideration or object is not lawful  if it is <u>prohibited by law,</u> 
- or it is such as <u>would defeat the provisions of law</u>, 
- if it is <u>fraudulent or involves injury</u> to the person or property of another
- or <u>court regards it as immoral</u> or <u>opposed to public policy</u>.”
#### Not Expressly [^3]declared to be void 

- The agreement entered into must not be which the law declares to be either<u> illegal or void. </u>
- An illegal agreement is an agreement <u>expressly or impliedly prohibited by law</u>. 
- A void agreement is one<u> without any legal effects</u>.

## Types of Contract 



### On the basis of Validity 


#### Valid Contract 
- “An agreement which is <u>binding[^4] and enforceable</u> is a valid contract. 
- It contains all the <u>essential elements of a valid contract.</u>”
#### Void Contract
Section 2 (j) states as follows:
- “A contract which <u>ceases to be  enforceable by law</u> becomes void 
- when it <u>ceases to be enforceable</u>”. 
- Thus, a void  contract is one which <u>cannot be enforced by a court of law.</u>

> [!attention]
> It may be added by way of clarification here that when a <u>contract is void, it is not a contract at all but for the purpose of identifying it, it has to be called a void contract.</u>”



#### Voidable Contrac
- Section 2(i) defines that “an agreement which is  <u>enforceable by law at the option of one or more parties</u> thereto, 
- but <u>not  at the option of the other or others</u> is a voidable contract”.

Following are the situations where a contract is voidable:
1. When the <u>consent of party is not free</u> is caused by coercion, undue  influence, misrepresentation or fraud.
2. When a person promises to do something for another person, but the  other person <u>prevents him from performing his promise,</u> the contract  becomes voidable at the option of first person.
3. When a party to a contract promise to perform a work within a  specified time, <u>could not perform with in that time</u>, the contract is  voidable at the option of promisee.

- Despite  this, there is similarity between them is that in both cases they are void <u>ab initio</u>[^5]  and <u>cannot be enforced by law</u>.

## PROPOSAL / OFFER  SECTION 2(a) OF THE INDIAN  CONTRACT ACT, 1872


### Proposal 
According to Section 2(a) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872,
- when one person <u>signifies to another</u> 
- his <u>willingness to do or to abstain</u> from doing anything 
- with <u>a view to obtaining the assent</u> of that other
- to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal


### Essentials of a Proposal and Offer are 

1. Promisor or Offeror , Offeree and promisee or acceptor
	- The <u>person making the proposal or offer</u> is called the ‘<u>promisor</u>’ or ‘<u>offeror</u>’
	- The <u>person to whom the offer is made</u> is called the ‘<u>offeree</u>’
	- and the <u>person accepting the offer</u> is called the ‘<u>promisee</u>’ or ‘<u>acceptor</u>’.
2. For a valid offer, the party making it must express his willingness ‘to do’ or ‘not to do’ something 
	- There must be an expression of willingness to do or not to do some act by the offeror.”
3. The willingness must be expressed <u>with a view to obtain the assent</u> of the other  party to whom the offer is made.
4. “An offer can be <u>positive</u> as well as <u>negative</u>: 
	- Thus<u> “doing” is a positive act </u>and <u>“not doing”, or “abstinence” is a negative act</u>; nonetheless both these acts have the same effect in the eyes of law.”


### Classification of Offer 
#### General offer 
- “It is an <u>offer made to public at large</u> and 
- hence anyone can accept and do the desired act [[#Carlill Vs. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893)]]
- In terms of Section 8 of the Act, anyone<u> performing the conditions of the offer can be considered to have accepted the offer</u>. 
- Until the general offer is retracted[^13] or withdrawn, it can be accepted by anyone at any time as it is a continuing offer.”


#### Special Offer 

- When the offer is <u>made to a specific</u> or <u>an ascertained person</u>, it is known as a specific offer. 
- Specific offer can be accepted only by that specified person to whom the offer has been made. [[#Boulton vs. Jones (1857)]]
#### Cross Offer 

- “When two parties<u> exchange identical offers </u>
- in ignorance at the time of each other’s offer, the offers are called cross offers. 
- There is <u>no binding contract</u> in such a case because offer made by a person<u> cannot be construed[^14] as acceptance of the another’s offer</u>.”

#### Counter Offer 

- When the offeree offers to <u>qualified acceptance</u> of the offer<u> subject to modifications and variations </u>in the terms of original offer, he is said to have made a counter offer. 
- Counter-offer <u>amounts to rejection of the original offer</u>. It is also called as<u> Conditional Acceptance</u>.”


#### Standing or continuing or open offer
- An offer which is <u>allowed to remain open for  acceptance over a period of time </u>is known as standing or continuing or open offer. 
- Tenders that are invited for supply of goods is a kind of standing offer.


### Essentials of Valid Offer 

#### It must be capable of creating legal relations
- “Offer must be such as in<u> law is capable of being accepted</u> and <u>giving rise to legal relationship</u>.
- If the offer does not intend to give rise to legal consequences and creating legal relations, it is not considered as a valid offer in the eye of law.
- A social invitation, even if it is accepted, does not create legal relations because it is not so intended.”


#### It must be certain, definite and not vague

- If the terms of an offer 
- are vague 
- or indefinite, 
- its acceptance cannot create any contractual relationship.”


#### It must be communicated to the offeree 

- “An offer, to be complete, must be communicated to the person to whom it is made, 
- otherwise there can be no acceptance of it. 
- Unless an offer is communicated, there can be no acceptance by it. 
- An acceptance of an offer, in ignorance of the offer, is not acceptance and does not confer[^15] any right on the acceptor.” [[#Lalman Shukla v. GauriDutt]]

#### It must be made with a view to obtaining the assent of the other party
- Offer must  be made with a view to obtaining the assent of the other party addressed
- and not  merely with a view to disclosing the intention of making an offer.


#### It may be conditional
- An offer can be made subject to any terms 
- and conditions by  the offeror.

#### Offer should not contain a term the non-compliance[^17]of which would amount to acceptance
- Thus, one cannot say that if acceptance is 
- not communicated by a certain time 
- the offer would be considered as accepted.


#### The offer may be either specific or general: 

- Any offer can be made to either public  at large or 
- to the any specific person. 


#### The offer may be express or implied: 
- An offer may be made 
	- either by words or
	- by conduct.


#### Offer is Different from a <u>mere statement of intention</u>, <u>an invitation to offer,</u> a  <u>mere communication of information</u>, <u>A prospectus and Advertisement</u>.

1. A statement of intention and announcement.
2. Offer must be distinguished from an answer to a question. [[#Harvey vs. Facie 1893 AC 552]]
3. A statement of price is not an offer. Quoting the price of a product does not  constitute it as offer
4. An invitation to make an offer or do business.In case of “an invitation to make  an offer”, the person making the invitation does not make an offer <u>rather invites  the other party to make an offer</u>. [[#Harris vs. Nickerson 1873]]
5. A statement of price is not an offer

### What is Invitation to offer 

- An offer should be distinguished from an invitation to offer. 
- An offer is definite  and capable of converting an intention into a contract. 
- Whereas an invitation to an  offer is only a circulation of an offer,
- it is an attempt to<u> induce offers and precedes a  definite offer</u>.


In order to ascertain whether a particular statement amounts  to an ‘offer’ or an ‘invitation to offer’, 
- the test would be intention with which such  statement is made.
- Does the person who made the statement intend to be bound by  it as soon as it is accepted by the other or 
- he intends to do some further act, before  he becomes bound by it. 
- In the former case, it amounts to an offer and in the latter  case, it is an invitation to offer.
Following are instances of invitation to offer to buy or sell: 
- (i) A Prospectus by a company to the public to subscribe for its shares. 
- (ii) Display of goods for sale in shop windows.  
- (iii) Advertising auction sales and  
- (iv) Quotation of prices sent in reply to a query regarding price.


### Offer vs Invitation of Offer 
| Basis                    | Offer                                                                 | Invitation to Offer                                                                                  |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Meaning                  | As per Section 2(a) of the Act, an offer is the final expression of willingness by the offeror to be bound by the offer should the other party choose to accept it. | Where a party, without expressing final willingness, proposes certain terms on which he is willing to negotiate; he does not make an offer but only invites the other party to make an offer on those terms. |
| Intention of the parties | If a person making the statement intends to be bound by it as soon as the other accepts, he is making an offer. | If a person has the intention of negotiating on terms, it is called an invitation to offer.           |
| Sequence                 | An offer cannot be an act precedent to invitation to offer.            | An invitation to offer is always an act precedent to offer.                                           |


## Acceptance 

- When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies 
- his assent thereto, proposal  is said to be accepted. 
- The proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise

Relationship between offer and acceptance: According to Sir William Anson 
- “<u>Acceptance  is to offer what a lighted match is to a train of gun powder</u>”. 
- The effect of this observation  is that what acceptance triggers cannot be recalled or undone.
- But there is a choice to the  person who had the train to remove it before the match is applied.
- It in effect means that the  offer can be withdrawn just before it is accepted. 
- Acceptance converts the offer into a promise  and then it is too late to revoke it.
- This means as soon as the train of gun powder is lighted it  would explode. 
- Train of Gun powder [offer] in itself is inert, 
- but it is the lighted match [the  acceptance] which causes the gun powder to explode. 
- The significance of this is an offer in  itself cannot create any legal relationship but it is the acceptance by the offeree which creates  a legal relationship. 
- Once an offer is accepted it becomes a promise and cannot be withdrawn  or revoked. 
- An offer remains an offer so long as it is not accepted but becomes a contract as  soon as it is accepted.

### Legal Rules Regarding a Valid Acceptance 

#### Acceptance can be given only by the person to whom offer is made: 
- In case of a  specific offer, it can be accepted only by the person to whom it is made. [[#Boulton vs. Jones (1857)]]
- In case of a general offer, it can be accepted by any person who has the knowledge of  the offer.[[#Carlill Vs. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893)]]


#### Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified: 
- As per section 7 of the Act,  acceptance is valid only when it is <u>absolute and unqualified</u> and is
- also expressed in  some<u> usual and reasonable manner</u> 
- unless the proposal prescribes the manner in which  it must be accepted. 
- If the proposal prescribes the manner in which it must be  accepted, then it must be accepted accordingly. [[#Neale vs. Merret (1930) W. N. 189.]] [[#Union of India vs. Bahulal AIR  1968 Bombay 294]]

#### The acceptance must be communicated: 
- To conclude a contract between the parties,  the acceptance must be communicated in some perceptible[^19] form. 
- Any conditional  acceptance or acceptance with varying or too deviant conditions is no acceptance. 
- Such conditional acceptance is a counter proposal and has to be accepted by the  proposer, if the original proposal has to materialize into a contract. [[#Brogden vs. Metropolitan Railway Co. (1877)]]
- A mere variation in the language not involving any difference in substance would not  make the acceptance ineffective. [[#Heyworth vs. Knight 1864 144 ER 120]]

#### “Acceptance must be in the prescribed mode: 
- Where the mode of acceptance is prescribed in the proposal, 
- it must be accepted in that manner. 
- But if the proposer does not insist on the proposal being accepted in the manner prescribed after it has been accepted otherwise, i.e., 
- not in the prescribed manner, the proposer is presumed to have consented to the acceptance.”

#### “Time:
- Acceptance must be given within the specified time limit, 
- if any, and if no time is stipulated, acceptance must be given within the reasonable time and before the offer lapses. 
- What is reasonable time is nowhere defined in the law and thus would depend on facts and circumstances of the particular case.”
#### Mere silence is not acceptance: 
- The acceptance of an offer <u>cannot be implied from  the silence</u> of the offeree or his failure to answer, 
- unless the offeree has in any previous  conduct indicated that his silence is the evidence of acceptance.
#### “Acceptance by conduct/Implied Acceptance:
- Section 8 of the Act lays down that 
	- “<u>the performance of the conditions of a proposal</u>[^20], or 
	- <u>the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise</u> [^21]
	- which may be offered with a proposal, 
	- constitutes[^22] an acceptance of the proposal. 
- This section <u>provides the acceptance of the proposal by conduct</u> as against other modes of acceptance i.e. verbal or written communication. 
- Therefore, <u>when a person performs the act</u> intended by the proposer<u> as the consideration for the promise</u> offered by him, the performance of the act <u>constitutes acceptance</u>.”

## Communication of Offer and Acceptance 


### Communication of Offer 

- the communication of offer is complete <u>when it comes to the knowledge of the person</u> to whom it is made. 
- Mere receiving of the letter is not sufficient, he <u>must receive or read the message contained</u> in the letter. 

### Communication of acceptance 

1. Communication by act 
	- by words 
		- would include any expression of words whether written or oral. 
		- Written words will include letters, telegrams, faxes, emails and even advertisements. 
		- Oral words will include telephone messages. 
	- by conduct
		- Again communication would include any conduct intended to communicate like positive acts or signs 
		- so that the other person understands what the person ‘acting ‘ or ‘making signs’ means to say or convey.
2. Communication of acceptance by ‘omission’ to do something. 
	- Such omission is <u>conveyed by a conduct</u> or <u>by forbearance</u> on the part of one person to convey his willingness or assent. 
	- However, silence would not be treated as communication by ‘omission’.


---

when communication of acceptance is complete. In terms of  Section 4 of the Act, it is complete,


(i) **As against the proposer**, when it is <u>put in the course of transmission to him</u> so as to  be out of the power of the acceptor to withdraw the same; 

(ii) **As against the acceptor**, when it <u>comes to the knowledge of the proposer</u>.


> [!info] Here ‘A’ the proposer will be bound by B’s acceptance,
> “even if the letter of acceptance is delayed in post or lost in transit. The golden rule is proposer becomes bound by the contract, the moment acceptor has posted the letter of acceptance. But it is necessary that the letter is correctly addressed, adequately stamped and duly posted. In such an event the loss of letter in transit, wrong delivery, non delivery etc., will not affect the validity of the contract.”


“**Acceptance over telephone or telex or fax:** When an offer is made of instantaneous communication like telex, telephone, fax or through e-mail, the contract is <u>only complete when the acceptance is received by the offeree</u>, and the contract is made at the place where the acceptance is received (Entores Ltd. v. Miles Far East Corporation).”


**Communication of special conditions:** Sometimes there are situations where there are contracts with special conditions. These special conditions are<u> conveyed tacitly</u> and the acceptance of these conditions are also conveyed by the offeree again tacitly[^23] or without him even realizing it.”




> [!Example] 
> Example 60: Where a passenger undertakes[^24] a travel, the conditions of travel are printed at  the back of the tickets, sometimes these special conditions are brought to the notice of the  passenger, sometimes not. In any event, the passenger is treated as having accepted the  special condition the moment he bought his ticket.


**Standard forms of contracts:** It is well established that a standard form of contract may be enforced on another who is <u>subjectively unaware</u> of the contents of the document, provided the party wanting to enforce the contract has given notice which, in the circumstances of a case, is sufficiently reasonable.


But the acceptor will not incur[^25] any contractual obligation, if  the document is so printed and delivered to him in such a state that it does not give  reasonable notice on its face that it contains certain special conditions.

## Revocation of Offer and Acceptance

(i) as against the person who makes it when it is put into a course of transmission to  the person to whom it is made so as to be out of the power of the person who makes  it, and 

(ii) as against the person to whom it is made, when it comes to his knowledge.


“The above law can be illustrated as follows: If you revoke your proposal made to me by a telegram, the revocation will be complete, as far as you are concerned when you have dispatched the telegram. But as far as I am concerned, it will be complete only when I receive the telegram.”

“Ordinarily, the offeror can revoke his offer before it is accepted. If he does so, the offeree cannot create a contract by accepting the revoked offer.”


In terms of Section 5 of the Act a proposal can be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer. An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of acceptance is complete as against the acceptor.

“An acceptance to an offer must be made before that offer lapses or is revoked.”


### Law India and England 

“The law relating to the revocation of offer is the same in India as in England, but the law relating to the revocation of acceptance is different.”

“In English law, the moment a person expresses his acceptance of an offer, that moment the contract is concluded, and such an acceptance becomes irrevocable, whether it is made orally or through the post.”


“Contract through post- As acceptance, in English law, cannot be revoked, so that once the  letter of acceptance is properly posted the contract is concluded

In Indian law, the acceptor or can revoke his acceptance any time before the letter of acceptance reaches the offeror, if the revocation telegram arrives before or at the same time with the letter of acceptance, the revocation is absolute.”

“Contract over Telephone- A contract can be made over telephone. The rules regarding offer and acceptance as well as their communication by telephone or telex are the same as for the contract made by the mutual meeting of the parties. The contract is formed as soon as the offer is accepted but the offeree must make it sure that his acceptance is received by the offeror, otherwise there will be no contract, as communication of acceptance is not complete. If telephone unexpectedly goes dead during conversation, the acceptor must confirm again that the words of acceptance were duly heard by the offeror.”

“Revocation of proposal otherwise than by communication: When a proposal is made, the proposer may not wait indefinitely for its acceptance. The offer can be revoked otherwise than by communication or sometimes by lapse.”

## Case Law 

### Carlill Vs. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893)

**Facts**: In this famous case, Carbolic smoke Ball Co. advertised in several newspapers  that a reward of £100 would be given to any person who contracted influenza after  using the smoke balls produced by the Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. according to printed  directions. One lady, Mrs. Carlill, used the smoke balls as per the directions of company  and even then, suffered from influenza. Held, she could recover the amount as by using  the smoke balls she had accepted the offer.


### Lalman Shukla v. GauriDutt

**Facts:** G (Gauridutt) sent his servant L (Lalman) to trace his missing nephew. He then  announced that anybody who traced his nephew would be entitled to a certain reward.  L traced the boy in ignorance of this announcement. Subsequently when he came to  know of the reward, he claimed it. Held, he was not entitled to the reward, as he did  not know the offer.

### Harvey vs. Facie 1893 AC 552


In this case, Privy Council succinctly[^18] explained the distinction between an offer  and an invitation to offer. In the given case, the plaintiffs through a telegram  asked the defendants two questions namely,  
(i) Will you sell us Bumper Hall Pen? and  
(ii) Telegraph lowest cash price.

The defendants replied through telegram that the “lowest price for Bumper Hall  Pen is £ 900”. The plaintiffs sent another telegram stating “we agree to buy  Bumper Hall Pen at £ 900”. However, the defendants refused to sell the property  at the price.  

The plaintiffs sued the defendants contending that they had made an offer to  sell the property at £ 900 and therefore they are bound by the offer. 

However, the Privy Council did not agree with the plaintiffs on the ground that  while plaintiffs had asked two questions, the defendant replied only to the  second question by quoting the price but reserved their answer with regard to  their willingness to sell. Thus, they made no offer at all. Their Lordships held  that the mere statement of the lowest price at which the vendor would sell  contained no implied contract to sell to the person who had enquired about  the price.
